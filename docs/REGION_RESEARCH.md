Senior/disabled households qualify for levy exemptions that cut the effective rate roughly 35–40% (the "with exemptions" column runs 5.12–5.72 mills in the 2026 report), relevant to a retirement purchase.

## ZONING & HOME BUSINESS
Clark County's rural zones are set out in **Clark County Code Title 40** (Unified Development Code, hosted at codepublishing.com; chapters amended through Ord. 2025-07-01). Key districts:

- **Rural residential — R-20, R-10, R-5:** minimum lot sizes **20 / 10 / 5 acres** respectively (Table 40.210.020-2). R-5 (5-acre min) is the most common "small acreage" zone in the northern county.
- **Resource lands — AG-20, FR-40, FR-80, AG-WL:** 20–80 acre agricultural/forest minimums; not realistic for this client but define the working-farm landscape around Amboy/Yacolt.
- **Rural center — RC-2.5, RC-1:** small-lot rural cluster zones.

**Home business (Sec. 40.260.100)** is the critical section for this client. Three tiers:
- **Exempt:** rural — up to **400 sqft** of accessory structure, ≤2 employees, no customers on site, no outside storage, one business vehicle.
- **Minor (Type I permit):** rural — up to **1,000 sqft** accessory structure, ≤3 non-resident employees, ≤6 customers/day, ≤2 business vehicles + 2 pieces of heavy equipment.
- **Major (Type II permit):** rural — accessory-structure allowance **scales with acreage** (Table 40.260.100-1): on **2.5–5 acres, up to 2,500 sqft**; 5–7.5 ac → 3,000; 7.5–10 → 3,500; 10–15 → 4,000; 15–20 → 4,500; ≥20 ac → 5,000 sqft. Non-resident employees up to 6; customer round-trips/day up to 12. **Minimum lot size 2.5 acres** for a major rural home business.

This maps almost exactly onto the client's ask: a **600–1,500 sqft shop/ADU** for production + storage + classroom/workshop is squarely within the minor (1,000 sqft) and major (2,500 sqft on 2.5–5 ac) tiers, and the 10–20 vehicle parking requirement is accommodated by the major tier's activity-area rules (activity area up to 2% of parcel on 5+ acres, screened to L3 landscape standard, 50-ft setback). **On-site retail is prohibited except "incidental" sales** — a genuine constraint for a product business wanting a showroom; product sales must route through e-commerce, wholesale, farmers-market, or the agricultural-market channel below.

**Outbuildings/ADU:** Detached accessory buildings are permitted outright in all rural single-family zones (Sec. 40.260.010; 18-ft height under 20,000 sqft lots, 35-ft on larger lots). **Rural Accessory Dwelling Unit (RADU, Sec. 40.260.022, amended Ord. 2024-03-01)** — up to **1,500 sqft**, allowed in RC-1/2.5, R-5/10/20, AG-20, FR-40/80, AG-WL; **one RADU per lot**; 75% reduction in school/transportation impact fees. Note: a RADU cannot coexist with a Type II (major) home business on the same lot (40.260.022(B)(3)) — so the client must choose between a permitted *dwelling* ADU vs. a business-occupied shop; a detached **shop/garage** used for the business (not as a dwelling) sidesteps this conflict.

**Cottage food & product manufacturing:** Washington's Cottage Food law (WSDA) permits low-risk prepared foods (baked goods, jams, dried goods) from a home kitchen with a permit and an annual gross-sales cap (the statutory limit is **$35,000/year** under current WSDA guidance; the agency's site moved and the exact page is JS-gated — confirm the current cap at agr.wa.gov before relying on it). For a production shop above cottage scale, Clark County routes the use through the home-business tiers above, plus Clark County Public Health food-establishment permitting for value-added food.

**Farm stands / agricultural markets (Sec. 40.260.025):** a **roadside farm stand** (≤1,000 sqft sales area) is exempt from land-use review if selling on-site-grown product; an **agricultural market** (agritourism, classes, harvest festivals, farm tours) is allowed with a permit, with building sizes up to 4,000–12,000 sqft scaling by farm size. **Agritourism and on-farm classes/workshops are explicitly permitted** — this directly supports the client's classroom/workshop use on an actively-farmed parcel.

**HOA prevalence:** Most of the client's target acreage (Yacolt, Amboy, eastern Hockinson, rural Battle Ground/La Center) is **unplatted or lightly-platted rural land with no HOAs** — a major advantage vs. Ridgefield/Brush Prairie, where newer master-planned subdivisions (and their CC&Rs/deed restrictions) are common. Brush Prairie in particular has gated and deed-restricted estate subdivisions; Ridgefield's growth areas are heavily HOA'd. Buyers must still check each parcel for recorded covenants, but the eastern rural towns are the low-HOA option.

## HEALTHCARE
All towns are within ~20–35 minutes of Vancouver's two hospital campuses:

- **PeaceHealth Southwest Medical Center** (Vancouver) — **450-bed** community hospital, **Level II trauma center**, founded 1858 (Wikipedia, current 2026). The dominant provider in Clark County (PeaceHealth is the county's largest employer, 4,908 employees — CREDC Top Employers, 2026).
- **Legacy Salmon Creek Medical Center** (Vancouver) — acute-care hospital opened 2005, part of Legacy Health, 1,200+ employees, 24/7 ER (Wikipedia, current 2026).
- **The Vancouver Clinic** — 2,305 employees (CREDC 2026), the region's large multi-specialty group with urgent-care sites; **Kaiser Permanente** (1,000 employees, CREDC 2026) operates Cascade Park and other clinics in east Vancouver.
- **Proximity:** Battle Ground and Brush Prairie are ~15–20 min to both hospitals; Hockinson ~15 min; Ridgefield and La Center ~15–20 min (both on/near I-5); Yacolt and Amboy are the longest haul at ~25–35 min to PeaceHealth/Legacy. Battle Ground has PeaceHealth and Vancouver Clinic clinic locations in-town; there is **no hospital in any of the client's named towns** — all acute/ER care is in Vancouver, which is acceptable for a retirement household but matters for the "30-minute ER" comfort threshold for Amboy/Yacolt in poor weather.

## INTERNET & SERVICES
North Clark County is mid-transition on broadband. Verified providers and status (FCC National Broadband Map fixed-broadband data + provider sites, 2025):

- **Ziply Fiber** (formerly Frontier) — the dominant fiber overbuilder expanding across Clark County, offering symmetric multi-gig (up to 2–10 Gbps) in Battle Ground, Ridgefield, and La Center in-city and progressively into rural Hockinson/Brush Prairie. Ziply's coverage map (ziplyfiber.com) is the authoritative per-address check.
- **Comcast Xfinity** — cable DOCSIS available in the denser towns (Battle Ground, Ridgefield) and along arterials; gigabit down / ~35 Mbps up.
- **CenturyLink/Lumen (now Quantum Fiber)** — DSL and selective fiber in parts of the county.
- **Clark Public Utilities** — the local PUD runs a fiber-to-the-premises buildout (business and residential); its broadband map (clarkpublicutilities.com) shows active and under-construction areas, strongest in Battle Ground/Ridgefield corridors.
- **Fixed wireless — T-Mobile and Verizon Home Internet** — available across most of the county and now the practical default for Yacolt/Amboy/Hockinson rural parcels where no fiber/cable reaches; typical 50–300 Mbps down. **Starlink** is the reliable fallback (and often the fastest option) for deep-rural Amboy/Yacolt addresses.

**Bottom line:** Battle Ground, Ridgefield, and La Center in-city parcels can get fiber or cable ≥1 Gbps. Rural Hockinson/Brush Prairie parcels are mixed. **Yacolt and Amboy are the weak spots** — expect fixed-wireless (T-Mobile/Verizon) or Starlink, not fiber, at most rural addresses there; this is the single biggest infrastructure tradeoff of going far-east for cheaper land. **Parcel delivery:** UPS, FedEx, and USPS all serve every named town daily; rural driveways off maintained county roads are the only reliability risk (winter snow on the Lewis River/east hills can delay 1–2 days). The I-5 requirement is cleanly met only by **Ridgefield and La Center** (both interchanges on I-5); Battle Ground is ~8–10 min to I-5 via SR-502, and Hockinson/Brush Prairie ~15 min; Yacolt/Amboy are 20–30+ min off the freeway.

## SMALL BUSINESS
- **Farmers markets:** the region has a mature direct-market economy — Battle Ground, Ridgefield, La Center and Vancouver all host seasonal farmers markets, and Portland's large market system is a 30-min drive for higher-value product. The **agricultural-market / farm-stand provisions (40.260.025)** plus permitted agritourism make on-farm retail and classes viable on an actively-farmed parcel.
- **Agritourism:** explicitly encouraged county-wide (corn mazes, pumpkin patches, farm tours, harvest festivals, classes are listed as allowed agritourism activities). Yacolt/Amboy's Lewis River / Yacolt Burn scenic country is already an agritourism and u-pick destination corridor.
- **Home-based business climate:** Clark County is broadly **permissive for rural home businesses** relative to peers — the tiered permit system scales accessory-structure size with acreage and explicitly allows up to 6 employees and 12 customer trips/day on 10+ acres. The main frictions are (a) the **no-retail-except-incidental** rule, (b) Type II review + private-road neighbor agreements for larger operations, and (c) Clark County Public Health and building/fire codes for food or workshop uses.
- **Local customer base & jobs for the daughter/son-in-law:** ~23,500 Battle Ground + ~16,100 Ridgefield + ~3,800 La Center + the Vancouver metro (~510,000 in Clark County, 2025 est.) provide a real local market for classes and products; Portland (2.5M metro) is a 30–40 min drive. Major regional employers (CREDC Top Employers, 2026) hiring in health/social-services, education, and manufacturing include **PeaceHealth (4,908), Vancouver Public Schools (3,300), Evergreen Public Schools (2,599), The Vancouver Clinic (2,305), Legacy Health (1,800), Clark County (1,570), ilani casino (1,480), City of Vancouver (1,480), Clark College (1,417), Bonneville Power Administration (1,367), Battle Ground School District (1,313), HP (1,000), Kaiser Permanente (1,000), and SEH America (950)** — a psychology BA maps to school-district, clinic, and social-services roles within a 15–35 min commute of most target towns.

## ENVIRONMENTAL RISK
- **Flood:** The named towns sit mostly on upland benches, but the **East Fork Lewis River** (through La Center and east of Ridgefield) and the **mainstem Lewis River valley** (Woodland/Amboy lowlands) carry FEMA-mapped 100-year floodplains. **La Center's low elevation (52 ft)** and its East Fork frontage make river-adjacent parcels there the highest flood-risk area in the client's set; Battle Ground (285 ft) and Ridgefield (259 ft) have localized floodplain along the East Fork and lower creeks. **Yacolt/Amboy** hills are generally above floodplain except narrow valley bottoms. Buyers should reject any parcel in FEMA Zone A/AE along the East Fork Lewis or Lewis River and confirm with Clark County MapsOnline FEMA overlay (2026).
- **Wildfire:** Clark County has a **wildland-urban interface (WUI)** designation across the east and north timbered hills — the **Yacolt Burn** (1902, the largest fire in WA state history) burned exactly through the Yacolt/Amboy country, and that terrain remains the county's primary wildfire-risk zone. DNR and Clark County WUI maps place Amboy and east-Yacolt parcels at moderate-to-high wildfire hazard; a **recreational burn ban is in effect for unincorporated Clark County as of Sept 2026** (clark.wa.gov banner), signaling current fuel conditions. Ridgefield, La Center, and Battle Ground valley parcels are low wildfire risk.
- **Landslide:** minor concern, concentrated on steep wooded slopes and river bluffs in the eastern hills and along the East Fork Lewis; flat valley parcels are negligible risk.
- **Home insurance (2025–2026):** Washington has seen rising premiums and selective **non-renewals in high-WUI zones**, echoing the Western wildfire-insurance pullback. Expect to pay more — and possibly need a surplus-lines carrier — for an Amboy/Yacolt wooded parcel than for a Battle Ground/La Center valley parcel. Confirm insurability *before* contracting on any far-east timbered property. **The low-risk sweet spot is valley/upland open ground in Battle Ground, Hockinson, and Ridgefield/La Center outskirts** — flood-check the river frontage, and wildfire risk is minimal.

## USDA LOAN ELIGIBILITY
USDA Rural Development's **Single-Family Housing Guaranteed/Direct** programs define "rural" by population and urban-boundary criteria on the **USDA Property Eligibility map** (eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov, updated annually). For Clark County as of the current map:
- **Eligible (rural):** **Yacolt, Amboy, and the unincorporated east/north rural area** (including rural Hockinson and Brush Prairie outside the urban growth boundary) qualify — these are the parcels where a USDA guaranteed or direct loan is realistically available.
- **Ineligible (non-rural / within urban boundary):** **Battle Ground, Ridgefield, and La Center incorporated city limits** and their surrounding urban growth areas generally fall **outside** the USDA rural designation (Ridgefield especially, given its 2020–2025 growth from 10,319 to 16,132 and UGB expansion).
- **Income limits** apply (the guaranteed program's moderate-income cap for Clark County runs roughly $110k–$120k for a 1–4 person household, 2025 limit schedule — confirm the current-year figure in the USDA income-limit lookup). For a retirement buyer on fixed income, USDA **Direct** (Section 502) can subsidize payments, but the property must be modest and in an eligible rural area — which again points to **Yacolt/Amboy** as the USDA-eligible corner of the client's target zone.

## BOTTOM LINE
**Affordability verdict:** The client's **$400,000 ceiling is below the median house price in every one of the eight named communities** (medians $552k–$779k). North Clark County has been appreciating ~13.7%/yr with 161% 10-year growth, and I-5-corridor towns (Ridgefield, La Center) are now effectively out of reach at $400k for improved acreage. The budget only works in the **eastern, USDA-eligible, unincorporated belt — Yacolt and Amboy** — and there it buys either older single-story/manufactured housing on 1–5 acres or raw acreage, not a move-in-ready shop estate.

**Best towns for this client:**
1. **Yacolt / rural east Hockinson** — cheapest acreage in the target zone, R-5/R-10 zoning with a permissive major-home-business tier (2,500 sqft shop on 2.5–5 ac, up to 5,000 sqft on 20+ ac), no HOA pressure, USDA-eligible, low violent crime; costs are fiber-less broadband (fixed-wireless/Starlink), 25–35 min to hospital, and elevated wildfire/insurance risk on timbered parcels — pick open upland, not forest.
2. **La Center** (unincorporated outskirts) — best I-5 access in the group, lowest flood-risk *if* kept off the East Fork frontage, low crime (8.4 violent), strong local/Portland market access; but in-city median is $627k, so target the surrounding R-5 county parcels (still near $400k only with an older home).
3. **Amboy** — deepest USDA rural eligibility and lowest entry price, but the farthest commute, highest wildfire exposure (Yacolt Burn terrain), and no fiber; only if the client accepts Starlink + 30-min ER.

**Worst fit:** **Brush Prairie** ($778k median, deed-restricted/gated estate subdivisions) and **Ridgefield** ($642k + heavy HOA/UGB growth + no USDA eligibility) — both priced and structured against a $400k rural home-business purchase.

**Sleeper:** **Rural Battle Ground (unincorporated, SD 98 Hockinson)** — city-level amenities, fiber from Ziply/Comcast, low taxes (8.19 mills), ~10 min to I-5, and pockets of 1–5 acre parcels below the city median; watch flood along the East Fork and expect competition. If a parcel clears the flood/WUI screen, this is the best balance of infrastructure and price-per-acre within striking distance of $400k.


**Agent top towns:** Yacolt / rural east Hockinson, La Center (unincorporated outskirts), Rural Battle Ground (SD 98 Hockinson)

## ZONING & HOME BUSINESS

Cowlitz County regulates unincorporated land under the **Land Use and Development Code, Chapter 18.10 (CCC 18.10)**, administered by the Building & Planning Division, which also applies the **Critical Areas Ordinance, Shoreline Master Program, and SEPA** review. Critically for this client, **only a portion of Cowlitz County is zoned** (county website, "Zoning" page) — large rural areas operate without a formal zoning district, which generally means fewer restrictions on agriculture, outbuildings, and accessory structures, but also requires a **planning clearance** review for any development. Development proposals are checked against the Comprehensive Plan and Land Use Ordinance regardless of whether a specific zone applies.

**Practical points for this purchase:** (1) **Minimum lot sizes** in rural/ag areas are typically 5–20 acres where zoned (agricultural and forest/resource districts), so a 1–5 acre parcel is more likely to fall in rural-residential or unzoned territory — verify on the county's **EPIC mapping** portal before offering. (2) **Home occupation permits** are available countywide for low-impact home businesses; **cottage-food and value-added food production** fall under Washington State cottage food law, and product manufacturing that exceeds home-occupation thresholds (noise, traffic, square footage) may require a conditional use permit or a shift to a commercial/light-industrial designation — a 600–1,500 sq ft production shop with 10–20 vehicle parking will likely exceed "home occupation" limits and need a **special/conditional use permit**. (3) **ADUs:** Washington's statewide ADU law (HB 1337, 2023, plus Commerce guidance) generally permits detached ADUs and requires local codes to allow them; confirm the county's adopted standards. (4) **HOAs/deed restrictions:** rare in rural Cowlitz County — most acreage is fee-simple without HOA — but always check the title commitment and any recorded covenants, especially in newer rural subdivisions and near the Clark County line. *(Sources: co.cowlitz.wa.us Zoning/Planning Clearance pages, retrieved Sep 2026; WA Dept. of Commerce HB 1337 guidance; MRSC ADU guidance Apr 2025.)*

## HEALTHCARE

**PeaceHealth St. John Medical Center (Longview)** is the regional anchor and — at **1,740 employees (2024)** — the single largest employer in the county (Wikipedia/Kelso top-employer table). This is a real but manageable commute for a retired couple; the daughter/son-in-law would also find the bulk of the county's healthcare jobs at PeaceHealth St. John itself. *(Sources: Wikipedia/Kelso employers 2024; PeaceHealth press releases Mar 2026.)*

## SMALL BUSINESS

## ENVIRONMENTAL RISK

**Flooding — the single biggest hazard, and a hard red flag.** The Cowlitz River is one of the region's most flood-prone systems. History: the original county seat **Monticello was destroyed by Cowlitz River flooding in December 1867**; the county's 2026 levy sheet contains **four separate flood/dike special-assessment districts** (Lexington Flood Upper/Lower, Dike 015 Willow Grove, Silver Lake Flood), a direct map of where flooding is recurrent and where buyers pay extra. **December 2025 brought a fresh, well-documented flood event** — a Cowlitz River flood warning in Kelso (Longview Daily News, Dec 9–11, 2025), Kalama residents wading through floodwater, and Washington-wide flooding with six rivers at dangerous levels (Washington Post, Dec 11, 2025); a **Cowlitz County road washed out Dec 12, 2025**. The Army Corps has since said **dredging most of the Cowlitz "is not a realistic option"** (The Daily Chronicle, Dec 16, 2025) and that Mount St. Helens sediment continues to raise flood risk (FOX 13 Seattle, Dec 18, 2025). **Kalama** is explicitly described in its own city entry as sitting on a "narrow floodplain." **Buyer guidance:** avoid valley-floor and river-adjacent parcels; verify every property's **FEMA flood zone** (and check whether it carries NFIP flood insurance) — target bench/foothill lots above the valley floor.

**Wildfire:** the county has **Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)** zones on its forested east side and foothills. Recent events include a **wildfire off I-5 in Kalama (Aug 21, 2025)** and a **Brush fire near Battle Ground (Aug 2026)**; the county also enforces seasonal burn bans (its website carried a live burn-ban banner in Sep 2026). Risk is moderate and localized — highest on timbered/steep slopes, not on cleared valley acreage. **Landslide:** steep slopes near the Toutle River and Mount St. Helens corridor carry landslide hazard; the Cowlitz River's Mount St. Helens sediment load (KATU, Jun 18, 2025) and Spirit Lake outflow-management questions (2025–26) are ongoing concerns for river-adjacent and Toutle Valley land. **Insurance:** flood insurance is mandatory in high-risk FEMA zones; wildfire-insurance surcharges and non-renewals are appearing in WA WUI areas. *(Sources: Wikipedia Longview/Kalama; Longview Daily News Dec 2025; Washington Post Dec 11 2025; KATU Aug 2025 & Jun 2025; co.cowlitz.wa.us burn ban.)*

## BOTTOM LINE

**Affordability verdict:** Cowlitz County is one of the last genuinely affordable rural-retirement pockets within an hour of Portland. A $400,000 budget buys a real single-story home on 1–5 acres — comfortably in Longview, Kelso, and Castle Rock; marginally in Kalama; and essentially **not** in Woodland (typical value ~$592,000). Flat-to-declining prices (metro median sale price −0.3% YoY) favor the buyer now, and property taxes are moderate (~$2,700–$4,100/yr). The county clears the client's hard requirements — I-5 access, fiber/Starlink broadband, UPS/FedEx/USPS, no meaningful HOA presence, agriculture-friendly (and largely unzoned) rural land, and real home-business latitude (with a conditional-use permit for a 600–1,500 sq ft production shop + 10–20 car parking).

**Two serious caveats override everything else:** (1) **Flood risk** — the Cowlitz River valley flooded in December 2025, four flood districts exist for a reason, and Monticello was literally wiped out by the river in 1867; the client must buy on high ground (bench/foothill lots) and verify the FEMA flood zone on every parcel. (2) **Economic shock** — the **May 2026 Nippon Dynawave mill implosion killed 11 workers** (the deadliest industrial tragedy in modern WA history), and **NORPAC was sold to International Paper for $360M (March–June 2026) as it exits the US newsprint market**, on top of Weyerhaeuser's long decline from 2,500 to 1,600 local jobs. This rattles the mill-town economy and matters for the daughter/son-in-law's job prospects, even though healthcare (PeaceHealth, 1,740 jobs) and the Port of Longview provide ballast.

**Best 2–3 towns for this client:**
1. **Castle Rock** — the sweet spot: $479k typical value but cheaper acreage on the fringe, I-5 access, average crime (38.7), above the worst flood zone, strong rural character, ~40 min to Longview jobs and ~1 hr to Portland.
2. **Kelso** — best value-to-services ratio ($386k typical value), immediate access to PeaceHealth (the county's #1 employer and a direct target for the daughter's psychology background in behavioral-health roles), Amtrak Cascades station for car-free Portland/Seattle commutes, though property crime is elevated in the urban core.
3. **Longview (foothill/bench lots only)** — most services and lowest prices ($385k), but avoid river-adjacent parcels and expect the highest property-crime profile.

**Worst choices:** **Woodland** (priced like Clark County — nearly $592k typical value, blows the budget), and any **Cowlitz River valley-floor / Kalama floodplain parcel** (flood-insurance and safety red flags). **Kalama proper** is safe and pretty but its floodplain setting and $540k price point undercut it for this brief.

**Sleeper towns inside the county:** **Toutle** (up 2.9% YoY, $504k typical value but larger, cheaper acreage; gateway to Mount St. Helens agritourism) and **Ryderwood** ($265k typical value — the affordability champion, but remote and weak on broadband/commute). **Ostrander and Silverlake** offer mid-priced acreage between Castle Rock and Longview with solid I-5 access and lower flood exposure than the valley floor.

## Lewis County, Washington (Centralia, Chehalis, Napavine, Winlock, Toledo + rural ~25mi)

# Lewis County, Washington — Rural Retirement & Home-Business Research Brief

*Prepared September 2026. All figures carry source name and data date. Housing/crime figures reflect BestPlaces aggregations as of their latest (2025–2026) update; census figures are 2020 with 2024 estimates where noted.*

## HOUSING MARKET

Lewis County is one of the most affordable rural-with-services corridors left on the I-5 axis between Seattle and Portland, though prices have climbed sharply off a low base. Per-town median home values (BestPlaces, retrieved Sept 2026):

| Town | Median home value | 1-yr appreciation | 10-yr appreciation | Median home age |
|---|---|---|---|---|
| **Winlock** | **$321,600** | 8.98% | 140.9% | 60 yrs |
| **Centralia** | $358,300 | 8.98% | 140.9% | 47 yrs |
| **Napavine** | $382,500 | 8.98% | 140.9% | 27 yrs |
| **Chehalis** | $445,200 | 8.98% | 140.9% | 64 yrs |
| **Toledo** | $475,800 | 8.98% | 140.9% | 59 yrs |

Caveat: BestPlaces reports an identical 8.98% one-year / 140.9% ten-year / 16.1% appreciation figure across all five towns, which indicates a county-level or template figure rather than distinct town trends — treat the *ordering* of median values as reliable and the appreciation as "roughly 9% county-wide over the last year." The direction is unambiguous: strong seller's market, values well above the 2010s. US median for comparison is $338,100 (BestPlaces).

For the client's **$400,000 ceiling with 1–5 usable acres**, the market segments cleanly:
- **Winlock and Napavine** are where $400k buys an actual acreage property. Winlock's $321,600 median with 60-year housing stock and 8.76 km² city footprint surrounded by rural Lewis County means a single-story older farmhouse on 1–5 acres regularly lists in the mid-$300ks–low-$400ks. Napavine's $382,500 median is offset by the *newest* housing stock in the county (median age 27 years) — the best value for a turnkey single-story ranch on acreage, but newer build means less acreage at the price ceiling.
- **Centralia** at $358,300 is the middle option, but in-town lots are small (city is only 7.62 sq mi land); 1–5 acre parcels with a house are found on the *rural fringe* (Hanaford Valley, Grand Mound, Adna), not in town.
- **Chehalis ($445,200) and Toledo ($475,800) are now above the $400k ceiling for a house-plus-acreage** unless the client buys a fixer or raw land. Toledo is the most expensive town in the county despite a population of only 631, driven by ToledoTel fiber (see Internet) and Cowlitz River frontage.

**Rural land price:** Lewis County raw acreage typically runs **$25,000–$45,000/acre** depending on timber vs. cleared pasture, well/septic status, and floodplain proximity (Lewis County assessor sale data, 2024 sales / 2025 assessments). Cleared, buildable 5-acre parcels with power at the road are at the high end; timbered or floodplain-adjacent parcels are at the low end. A 1–5 acre *with a single-story house* under $400k is realistic in Winlock/Napavine/rural fringe, tight in Centralia, and effectively out of reach in Chehalis/Toledo at today's prices.

## PROPERTY TAX

Washington has no state income tax; property tax is the major carrying cost. Lewis County's average **effective property tax rate is 0.65%** — median home $437,300 pays ~**$2,845/year** (SmartAsset Washington property-tax table, retrieved Sept 2026; county-level figure). This is among the lowest in the state, far below Pierce (0.88%), King, and Snohomish.

Structure and mechanics (Lewis County Assessor, "Property Tax Levy Information," accessed Sept 2026): the county comprises **169 tax code areas (TCAs)** across 60 districts — state, county, city, cemetery, fire, school, port, and library — carrying **92 separate levies**. A property's rate is the sum of the districts its TCA supports, so identical homes a mile apart can differ materially based on fire/school/library district. The Assessor publishes a **"2026 Levy Rates and Amounts"** page (and a 5-Year Levy Comparison, "2022 vs 2026") reflecting the 2026 collection-year levies certified in November 2025 (Resolution 25-340/25-341/25-342, adopted Nov 26, 2025). For a $400k rural property, expect roughly **$2,400–$3,200/year** depending on TCA, with rural (non-city) parcels generally at the low end because they don't pay a city levy.

Two client-relevant programs: (1) **Senior/Disabled Persons Exemption** freezes/limits property tax for qualifying over-61 homeowners with income caps (Assessor page); (2) **Current Use (open space / agricultural / timber) classification under RCW 84.34** can cut taxes dramatically on the acreage portion used for farming or timber — relevant since the client intends gardening/agricultural use. Budget roughly $250/month for taxes.

## ZONING & HOME BUSINESS

This is Lewis County's strongest selling point for the client's use case. The county's **Comprehensive Plan Land Use Element** (adopted, accessed Sept 2026) and **Title 17 (Land Use and Development Regulations)** are explicitly pro-rural-home-business.

- **Minimum lot size / density:** Rural residential policy **RL 7.1** states the county will "Maintain lot patterns that are typically **(5) five acres in size or larger**, except in subareas where historic lot patterns may be smaller." The implementing rural zone is the **Rural Development District (RDD)**. Policy **RL 7.2** expressly allows "a wide range of uses including residential, farming, forestry, **cottage industries, home occupations** and similar uses that do not rely on urban level of services." So a 1–5 acre parcel outside an Urban Growth Area is squarely within the intended rural character — 5 acres is the *typical* (not the minimum in all subareas) pattern.
- **Home occupation / cottage industry:** Rural economic-development policies **RL 2.1 and RL 2.2** specifically promote "jobs in agriculture, mining, timber production, **home occupations**, small businesses, ecotourism, recreation, energy production, agritourism, manufacturing, production of goods and **other cottage industries**" and "Encourage home occupations that provide goods and services to rural areas." This is a near-verbatim endorsement of the client's product-production + classroom/workshop plan.
- **Outbuildings / shop / ADU:** Rural Small Communities policy **RL 4.2** explicitly names "**accessory dwelling units**, duplexes, townhomes and cottage housing" among affordable housing types to be encouraged. The RDD zone carries cottage-industry and supportive-agricultural allowances (Comprehensive Plan, Table 8 — "Rural Residential… cottage industries and supportive agricultural uses"). A 600–1,500 sq ft shop/ADU for production/storage/workshop is a routine, permitted accessory use in RDD — subject to standard county setback, septic (on-site wastewater under RL 3.3), and stormwater rules rather than any prohibition. Note: **on-site septic (O.S.S.) sizing is the real constraint** — a workshop/classroom that draws 10–20-vehicle events may need a commercial or larger septic capacity, and Lewis County Environmental Health governs that, not zoning.
- **Home-business parking:** Parking 10–20 vehicles on a rural acreage parcel is unremarkable under RDD; no urban-style parking-minimum regime applies in rural zoning.
- **Cottage food / product manufacturing:** Washington's **Cottage Food Operation** law (RCW 69.22) permits most non-hazardous foods (baked goods, jams, dry goods) from a home kitchen with a simple permit and ≤$35k–$45k gross annual sales, no commercial kitchen — county doesn't block this. Beyond cottage scale, a home product-manufacturing use falls under home-occupation/cottage-industry allowances rather than an industrial zone, so long as it stays below thresholds that would push it into a Rural Area Industrial (RAI) classification.
- **HOA / deed restrictions:** HOA prevalence in rural Lewis County is **low** — the overwhelming majority of acreage parcels are unencumbered fee-simple rural lots with no HOA. The exceptions are a handful of newer platted subdivisions and gated riverfront developments (notably around Toledo/Cowlitz frontage and some Mayfield/Riffe lake communities), where CCRs *can* restrict outbuildings and home businesses. The client should verify title for CCRs on any specific parcel, but the default in this county is "no HOA, no deed restriction."
- **Avoidance note:** The Comprehensive Plan *prohibits* development within floodways (NE 6.5) and discourages it in the 100-year floodplain; the client's "avoid flood risk" requirement aligns with, not against, county policy.

## HEALTHCARE

The county's healthcare anchor is **Providence Centralia Hospital**, a 133-bed, Level IV trauma-designated facility in Centralia (Wikipedia "List of hospitals in Washington," retrieved Sept 2026) — the **largest employer in Lewis County at 900 staff** (Lewis County "Top employers, 2024," Wikipedia). Providence Centralia is part of the Providence Health & Services system (Catholic). The hospital operates the region's emergency department and a broad primary/surgical base; a second Providence hospital, Providence St. Peter (Olympia, Thurston County), is the level-II trauma referral ~30–40 minutes north.

For the client's needs:
- **Routine, ER, and many specialties are local.** The hospital plus Providence's Centralia/Chehalis outpatient clinics handle primary care, general surgery, imaging, and a range of specialists in-town.
- **Subspecialty care commutes north to Olympia/Providence St. Peter** or south toward Kaiser/PUH in Vancouver/Portland. Lewis County has no Level I/II trauma center, no academic medical center, and limited oncology/cardiology depth; complex or tertiary cases route to Olympia (~25 min from Centralia via I-5) or Portland (~90 min). Ambulance service is provided by **Medix Ambulance under county contract** (replacing AMR) (Lewis County Wikipedia).
- **Access reality for a rural retiree:** For a single-story retirement home, the meaningful gap is *specialist availability*, not primary care. A retiree with cardiology/oncology needs will be a regular Olympia commuter; a generally healthy retiree can get essentially everything locally. Budget the I-5 Olympia drive as a routine part of healthcare planning.

## SMALL BUSINESS

Lewis County's business climate is explicitly agriculture- and cottage-industry-friendly:
- **Farmers markets:** The **Chehalis Community Farmers Market** runs **June–October, Tuesday afternoons, on Boistfort Street in the historic downtown**, continuously since 2005 and celebrated its **20th anniversary in May 2025** (The Chronicle, May 28, 2025). It is the *only* farmers market within a 20-mile radius (Chehalis Wikipedia) — a meaningful distribution outlet for a client selling garden/product goods with little competition.
- **Southwest Washington Fair:** Held annually at the **Southwest Washington Fairgrounds in Chehalis** (Chehalis Wikipedia; fair historically runs the second week of August). It's the region's anchor agricultural/livestock event and a direct retail/agritourism opportunity.
- **Agritourism & cottage industries are policy priorities** (Comprehensive Plan RL 2.1, RL 5.x recreation/tourism LAMIRDs), and NRL 6.3 explicitly allows "small business and agritourism ventures" on resource lands.
- **Business climate / workforce context:** The county is a **leading US Christmas-tree producer (548,000 trees in 2022, 6th nationally)** and produces 600,000 tons of dried timber annually across 10 pulpwood plants, earning the state's first **AA Bioeconomy Development Opportunity (BDO) Zone** rating (Lewis County Wikipedia, 2024). It was selected for the **Pacific Northwest Hydrogen Hub (PNW H2)** in late 2023. These signal an active rural-economic-development posture, not a stagnant one. Wages run *below* state averages (median annual wage >$48k, household income <$59k, 2019–2020) — good for hiring low-cost help, but it's a low-wage market.

## ENVIRONMENTAL RISK

This is the client's single most important due-diligence item, and Lewis County has a *real, concentrated* flood problem:

- **The Chehalis River flood risk is the defining hazard.** The December 2007 **Great Coastal Gale** produced 22 inches of rain in the Upper Chehalis Basin; the **Great Coastal Gale of 2007** (Wikipedia) closed **I-5 near Chehalis for several days under ~10 feet of water** (a 4-hour/280-mile detour) and caused an estimated **$930 million** in damages (state commission estimate, Flood history in Chehalis Wikipedia). **Approximately 70% of Chehalis city limits sit in the floodplain**, and **2,700+ structures in Centralia** are in a comparable floodplain (Flood history in Chehalis, Wikipedia).
- **It is not historical — it is ongoing.** In **December 2025**, flooding on the Cowlitz and Newaukum rivers again triggered rescues and road/school closures across Lewis County, isolating the town of Randle (The Chronicle Dec 10, 2025; KOMO News Dec 9, 2025). The 2025 Pacific Northwest floods (Wikipedia) document repeated Lewis County inundation. The Chehalis Basin flood risk has risen ~33% since the 1980s, and a 2022 climate study (Office of the Chehalis Basin + UW) projects **14 major floods per century rising toward 45 per century** by mid-to-late century.
- **FEMA zones & mitigation:** The county regulates flood hazards under **LCC 15.35 and 17.38** (Comprehensive Plan NE 7.2), prohibits development in floodways (NE 6.5), and participates in the **Chehalis Basin Strategy / Chehalis River Basin Flood Authority** (non-dam/LAND alternatives). **Implication for the client:** *buy high, never in the floodplain.* The floodplain is concentrated along the Chehalis, Newaukum, and Skookumchuck river bottoms and the "Wet Prairie" fairgrounds area. Upland acreage in Napavine, Winlock, Toledo's higher benches, and the Centralia/Chehalis hill fringes is largely outside the Special Flood Hazard Area — but **verify the specific parcel's FEMA FIRM panel and elevation certificate before purchase**, because floodplain proximity in this county is the #1 thing that will wreck an insurance quote or resale.
- **Wildfire:** Lewis County has moderate wildfire/WUI (wildland-urban interface) risk on its east-side forested uplands (toward Randle/Packwood/Gifford Pinchot) but **low-to-moderate risk in the western valley towns** where the client is looking. The Comprehensive Plan addresses wildfire (NE 10) primarily as a forest-edge concern. Not a primary disqualifier for Winlock/Napavine/Toledo.
- **Landslide / geologic hazard:** Geologic-hazard regulation (NE 3.x) targets steep slopes and the Willapa Hills; the flat-to-rolling valley and bench parcels are low-risk. Avoid steep-slope parcels, which trigger geotechnical-study requirements.
- **Insurance:** Homeowners insurance is generally available and affordable in the valley towns; the acute insurance risk is confined to **flood-prone parcels requiring the NFIP** (mandatory in the SFHA with a federally backed mortgage). Chehalis flood-insurance costs run **almost three times Centralia's** per-structure (Flood history in Chehalis, Wikipedia). **Bottom line: buy a non-floodplain upland parcel and insurance is a non-issue.**

## USDA LOAN ELIGIBILITY

The client's sub-$400k rural purchase is a strong candidate for a **USDA Rural Development Single-Family Housing Direct/Guaranteed loan** (0% down, income-capped, subsidized for low/moderate income). Eligibility is by population threshold: areas ≤35,000 not part of a large metro urbanized area qualify. **Centralia (18,183 in 2020) and Chehalis (7,439)** anchor a small **Centralia micropolitan area**, and the surrounding unincorporated towns — **Napavine (1,888), Winlock (1,472), Toledo (631)** — are well under every threshold. The practical rule: **the in-town Centralia/Chehalis census-designated urbanized core may be USDA-ineligible, but the surrounding acreage and the small towns are eligible.** (USDA eligibility map at eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov — I could not load the live 2026 map this session, so verify the specific parcel's address on that portal; the population figures above are the determining inputs and are all comfortably rural.) The client should also compare USDA Direct (income-limited, lowest rate) vs. Guaranteed (higher income limit, lender-backed) given a retirement-income profile. Note USDA Direct income limits are strict; a two-earner household (daughter/son-in-law) could exceed them and route to Guaranteed or conventional.

## BOTTOM LINE

## PROPERTY TAX

Oregon caps property taxes via **Measure 5 (1990)** — total education + general-government rate cannot exceed $5 (schools) + $10 (non-school) per $1,000 of real market value — and **Measure 50 (1997)** — assessed value (AV) growth capped at 3%/year, with AV reset to 1995–96 levels for existing property.

- **Marion County:** median property tax **$2,049/yr** on a median AV of $205,100, ≈ **1.0% of assessed value** (tax-rates.org, "Marion County Property Tax Rate 2026" page; data set 2024–2025).
- **Polk County:** median property tax **$2,193/yr** on a median AV of $227,900, ≈ **0.96% of assessed value** (tax-rates.org, "Polk County Property Tax Rate 2026" page).
- **State average:** effective rate **0.78%** (SmartAsset Oregon Property Tax Calculator, accessed Sept 2026).
- Marion County Assessor confirms a 2025 certified assessed-value base of **$34.98 billion** across ~132,000 accounts (Marion County Assessor, "2025 certified data," accessed Sept 2026).

**Practical note:** Because Measure 50 caps AV growth at 3%, a newly purchased property's tax is re-figured at market value at sale, so a $400,000 acreage purchase will pay **~$3,900–$4,200/yr** in combined taxes (roughly $9.75–$10.50/$1,000) depending on the code-area levies, not the county *median* figures above. Oregon has no sales tax; income tax tops at 9.9% (the relevant offset for a retirement/hobby-business budget).

## ZONING & HOME BUSINESS

**The single most important decision for this client is avoiding Oregon's EFU (Exclusive Farm Use) zone for a non-farming home business.** EFU permits a dwelling only "in conjunction with farm use," gated by statutory income thresholds.

**Oregon EFU dwelling income rules (state law):**
- **ORS 215.213(1)(a):** primary farm dwelling allowed if the farm operation/woodlot is **20+ acres** and at least the county average size producing **≥$2,500/yr gross** (Oregon.public.law, accessed Sept 2026).
- **ORS 215.213(1)(b):** on a *smaller* tract, a dwelling is allowed if the parcel produced **≥$20,000/yr gross farm income** in 2 of the last 3 years (or is planted in perennials capable of averaging $20,000/yr) (Oregon.public.law, accessed Sept 2026).
- **ORS 215.279:** the income standard may be met by earning the required amount in **3 of the last 5 years, each of the last 2 years, or the best-3-of-5 average** (Oregon.public.law, accessed Sept 2026).

**For a retirement home with a *product-production* business (not commercial farming), the correct zones are the acreage-rural-residential districts, which allow single-family dwellings by right with no farm-income test:**

**Polk County (Polk County Zoning Ordinance, accessed Sept 2026):**
- **AR-5 (Acreage Residential–5 acre)** — the client's sweet spot. Permits single-family dwelling by right, plus accessory buildings, greenhouses, hothouses/hobby houses, private garages/shops. **Minimum 5 acres.** (PCZO Ch. 128.500, "2026 update.")
- **AR-10** (10-acre) and **AF-10** (Agriculture/Forestry 10-acre) — larger alternatives.
- **SR (Suburban Residential)** — smaller lots, tighter home-business limits.
- **EFU (Ch. 136)** — farm-use only; avoid for non-farm business.
- **Home Occupations (Ch. 116):** a *services*-only home occupation is by right in AR-5 (no employees, no commodities sold, ≤½ one-story floor area). **Commodity production** is a *conditional use* ("Home Occupation–Conditional," ≤½ house floor area, accessory building "residential in appearance"). A **Cottage Industry Home Occupation** (production in a detached building) is a conditional use in AR-5 allowing **up to 5 employees** and a 2-sq-ft nameplate sign — this is the mechanism for the client's 600–1,500 sq ft production/workshop building. *No retail trade on-site* is permitted (off-site sales/shipping OK). (PCZO Ch. 116, 2016 update.)
- **ADUs (Ch. 118, 2026 update):** allowed in AR-5. **Inside a UGB:** detached ADU ≤ **900 sq ft or 75% of main dwelling**, ≤25 ft tall. **Outside a UGB** (ORS 215.495): requires **≥2-acre parcel**, ≤900 sq ft, ≤100 ft from the main dwelling, plus a fire-district evacuation plan. This covers the client's 600–1,500 sq ft ADU *only up to 900 sq ft* — the larger "classroom/workshop" square footage must instead be pursued as a cottage-industry accessory building.
- Polk also has a **Wineries/Cider/Farm Breweries (Ch. 117)** chapter and **Agri-Tourism** provisions.

**Marion County (Marion County Code Title 17 "Rural Zoning," accessed Sept 2026):**
- **AR (Acreage Residential)** — minimum lot size **2 acres** (or the numeric suffix, e.g., AR-5 = 5 acres); single-family dwelling and "limited home occupations" by right (MCC 17.128.020, 17.128.070).
- **RA (Residential Agriculture)** — rural-residential with farming allowed.
- **EFU (17.136)** — new farm parcels minimum **80 acres**; non-farm parcels "only as large as necessary for the use"; dwellings gated by ORS 215 income standards (MCC 17.136.090).
- **SA (Special Agriculture), FT (Farm/Timber), TC (Timber Conservation)** — resource zones, avoid.
- **Home Occupations:** governed by **Ordinance #1204** (Home Occupations & Mobile Food Vendors), which sets the county's home-occupation standards (Marion County Planning, accessed Sept 2026).
- **Agri-tourism:** Marion County maintains a dedicated Agri-Tourism program page under Planning (Marion County, accessed Sept 2026).

**HOA/deed-restriction prevalence:** Acreage parcels in Polk/Marion are overwhelmingly **non-HOA** (individual taxlots, not platted subdivisions). HOAs cluster in city-limit subdivisions (Monmouth/West Salem new construction, Keizer). Rural acreage homesites rarely carry deed restrictions, but always pull the title report — some older "ranchette" partitions carry farm-adjacent deed covenants.

No extreme earthquake/liquefaction exclusion unique to the area beyond standard Willamette Valley expectations.

Benton County 2–5 acre rural-residential homes run **$450k–$650k** given higher land values and Corvallis demand — most exceed budget. **Rural land $/acre:** Willamette Valley small-acreage (non-EFU, RR-zoned) tracts commonly list **$40k–$90k/acre**; buildable EFU/exempt or RR-2 parcels on flatter farmland trend lower in Linn ($25k–$60k/acre) than Benton. (LandWatch/regional listings, 2025–26.)

## PROPERTY TAX

Oregon is levy-based (Measure 5/50 caps), so "rates" are expressed as an effective rate against market value. **Linn County effective rate: 0.82%** — on the median $417,300 home that's **~$3,409/year**. **Benton County effective rate: 0.89%** — on the median $566,100 home that's **~$5,014/year** (SmartAsset Oregon Property Tax Calculator, 2026 data). Both are below the 0.89% national average that SmartAsset cites; Oregon's constitutional limits cap general-government tax at $10/$1,000 and education at $5/$1,000 of *real market value* (Oregon Constitution Measure 5; SmartAsset 2026). Key client implication: a $400,000 Linn County rural property taxes at roughly **$3,200–$3,600/yr** before special farm-use assessment; a comparable Benton property at ~$3,500–$4,000/yr. Farm/forest land under special assessment (ORS 308A) pays dramatically less, but triggers a 5-year rollback penalty if the use changes (Linn County Assessor Special Assessment page, 2026).

## ZONING & HOME BUSINESS

This is the decisive section for the client's shop/ADU/classroom requirement. Oregon's statewide Goal 3 confines true farm use to **EFU (Exclusive Farm Use)** zones, where a *new dwelling* requires a demonstrated farm income threshold.

**EFU farm-dwelling income requirement (statewide, applies in both counties):** OAR 660-033-0135 requires, for a primary farm dwelling on **high-value farmland**, **$80,000 gross annual income from farm product sales in 2 of the last 5 years**; on **non-high-value** land the threshold drops to **$40,000 gross** (Oregon Administrative Rules 660-033-0135 and 660-033-0130, current). This is the "income threshold" the client must understand: **buying raw EFU acreage and putting up a new house + shop is only legal if you're a genuine commercial farmer hitting $40k–$80k gross.** A retirement/gardening/hobby property does NOT qualify for a new dwelling in EFU. Critically, **Linn County's Special Assessment guidance explicitly requires this income proof for EFU dwellings** (Linn County Assessor, ORS 215.203/215.213 page, 2026).

**Benton County zoning (Benton County Development Code, June 30 2018):**
- **EFU (Chapter 55):** minimum parcel **80 acres** for farm parcels; home occupation allowed but **max 5 employees**, operated by a resident, "substantially in the dwelling or associated buildings"; accessory dwelling only for persons "principally engaged in farm use"; non-farm residential parcels allowed only via strict criteria (no series partitions). EFU is a poor fit for a 1–5 acre home-business.
- **RR-2 / RR-5 / RR-10 / RR-20 (Chapter 63):** rural-residential; minimum parcel set by the suffix (**2, 5, 10, 20 acres**). **RR-2 = 2-acre minimum, RR-5 = 5-acre minimum** — these are exactly the 1–5 acre home-site zones the client wants. Permitted uses: one dwelling, farm/forest use, residential home (adult foster), day care <13 children, and a manufactured dwelling as a temporary hardship. Setbacks: 25' front, 8' side, 25' rear; dwelling must sit 300' from a resource zone; fire-retardant roof + spark arrestors within 200' of forestland. **This is the client's zone in Benton County.** Note: RR-2 and RR-5 designations cannot be *newly created* without a Goal 14 exception, so existing RR parcels are the supply.
- **Accessory structures/shop:** siting standards (63.405) permit detached non-residential buildings up to 500 sq.ft. with reduced 3' side/rear setbacks and 20' height; larger shops/ADUs go through standard review. A 600–1500 sq.ft. production shop + classroom is achievable in RR via conditional/limited use or as accessory structures.

**Linn County zoning (Linn County Land Development Code, Title 9 Subtitle 2):** zones include **EFU, Farm/Forest (F/F), Forest, RR (Rural Residential), and AR (Agricultural/Resource)** designations (Linn County "Division 3 – Zoning Districts," linncountyor.gov). Linn's RR/AR zones carry minimum lot sizes on the same 2–5–10-acre pattern as Benton. Linn is generally **more permissive and less expensive** than Benton for the same RR/AR designation. Exact per-district minimums live in the LDC (page is Cloudflare-gated; see "Bottom Line" note to verify by phone).

**Home occupation / cottage food / product manufacturing / classes:**
- **Home occupation** is broadly permitted in both counties' RR and (to a limited, farm-tied extent) EFU zones, with employee caps (Benton EFU: 5; RR typically similar) and "resident-operated" requirements (BCC 91.205–91.230; BCC 55).
- **Cottage food** (non-hazardous baked/jarred goods from a home kitchen) is legal under Oregon's cottage food law (ORS 616.695; ODA) without a commercial kitchen — directly supports a product line.
- **Classes/events** in a home shop fall under "accessory/incidental use" or require a conditional use permit (e.g., day care center, school, or commercial kennel are *conditional* in Benton RR — 63.205). A workshop/classroom teaching a few students at a time typically needs a **conditional use permit** in RR; verify against the specific LDC before purchase.
- **Parking 10–20 vehicles** for classes/events is the biggest soft spot: RR zones do not contemplate commercial parking volumes, so the client should confirm with planning staff and expect a conditional-use or limited-use approval.

**HOA / deed-restriction prevalence:** Rural Linn and Benton acreage is almost entirely **outside HOAs**; the HOA risk concentrates in newer in-town subdivisions (Albany/Millersburg/North Albany planned communities). Rural RR/AR/EFU parcels are typically fee-simple with no CC&Rs — but always order a title report; some former timber/farm tracts carry recorded deed restrictions or conservation easements.

## SMALL BUSINESS

- **Farmers markets:** Albany (downtown), Corvallis (Saturday market, one of the state's largest), Lebanon, and Philomath all run seasonal farmers markets — a direct retail channel for cottage-food or craft product lines.
- **Agritourism** is a growing Willamette Valley sector (u-pick, farm stands, tasting rooms); Linn County's flatter farmland and lower land cost make it a cheaper agritourism launch pad than Benton's wine-country prices.
- **OSU influence:** Oregon State University (Corvallis, ~30,000 students + ~7,000 staff) anchors the economy — demand for services, housing, and specialty food/craft, plus the **Food Innovation Center** and Extension programs that help small food producers. Linn-Benton Community College (Albany) adds workforce training.
- **Business climate:** Oregon has no sales tax (retail advantage) but a high personal income tax (up to ~9.9%); Oregon's business/tax structure is mid-pack nationally. Rural Linn County is broadly pro-development relative to Benton's stricter land-use posture.

## ENVIRONMENTAL RISK

- **Flood:** The **Willamette River** runs along Albany/Corvallis' western edge, with the **Calapooia** (through Albany/Sweet Home) and **South Santiam** (Lebanon/Sweet Home) as secondary systems. FEMA 100-year floodplains follow these corridors — **low-lying parcels near the rivers are in Zone AE/A and require flood insurance.** The client must check any candidate parcel against the FEMA Flood Map Service Center; avoid river-adjacent bottomland. Higher-bench farmland and the RR hills east of the valley floor are largely out of the floodplain. (Recent 2025 Pacific Northwest atmospheric-river floods affected Willamette Valley lowlands — Wikipedia "2025 Pacific Northwest floods," Jan 2026.)
- **Wildfire / WUI:** The **Cascades foothills east of Lebanon and Sweet Home** (and the Coast Range west of Corvallis) carry **Wildland-Urban Interface (WUI)** designation with real fire-insurance pricing risk; Oregon's 2020 Labor Day fires burned in Santiam Canyon just east of Sweet Home. Flat valley-floor farmland is low wildfire risk. Avoid heavily forested foothill lots if wildfire insurance cost is a concern.
- **Landslide:** Low risk on the flat valley floor; moderate on steep Coast Range/Cascades slopes.
- **Insurance:** Oregon has seen insurer pullback in high-WUI and high-flood zones. A valley-floor, non-floodplain Linn County parcel is the most insurable profile; foothill/forest parcels may require the Oregon FAIR Plan (state insurer of last resort). Confirm a firm quote before committing.

## USDA LOAN ELIGIBILITY

USDA Single Family Housing (Direct + Guaranteed) eligibility is set by population and rural designation (USDA Income & Property Eligibility Site, eligibility.sc.egov.usda.gov, 2026). In this market:
- **Eligible (rural):** rural Linn County, rural Benton County, and the smaller incorporated towns — **Lebanon (portions), Jefferson, Tangent, Scio, Sweet Home, Harrisburg, Halsey, Shedd, Millersburg, Brownsville, Philomath (portions), Monroe.** Most 1–5 acre rural parcels the client is considering fall in eligible territory.
- **Generally ineligible (urbanized):** **Albany, Corvallis**, and Lebanon's denser core are above the rural population threshold; in-town homes there do not qualify.
- USDA **income limits** (Guaranteed program) for Oregon run roughly **$110k–$120k for 1–4 person households** (varies by county; confirm the current-year figure at rd.usda.gov). The client must still meet standard credit/income underwriting, and the 1–5 acre parcel must not be "income-producing" farm (which would push toward a farm-loan product instead). USDA's 0%-down, 100% LTV rural financing is a strong lever for the sub-$400k acreage search in Linn/Benton.
